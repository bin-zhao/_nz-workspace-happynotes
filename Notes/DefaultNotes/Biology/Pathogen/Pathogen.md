What is pathogenicity?

**Pathogenicity** is the ability of a microorganism, such as a bacterium, virus, or fungus, to cause disease. It is a qualitative trait, meaning an organism either has the potential to cause disease or it does not. Pathogenicity is distinct from **virulence**, which refers to the _degree_ or _severity_ of the disease caused by the organism. For example, one strain of a pathogenic bacteria might be more virulent than another, causing a more severe illness, but both are still pathogenic. 

Factors affecting pathogenicity

The ability of an organism to cause disease depends on a variety of factors: 

- **Presence of virulence factors:** Pathogenic organisms possess specific genes or traits that increase their ability to cause disease. These might include toxins, enzymes that damage host tissues, or mechanisms for evading the host's immune system.
- **Host susceptibility:** An organism's pathogenicity also depends on the host's immune status and genetic makeup. A microorganism that is typically harmless can become pathogenic in an immunocompromised individual.
- **Route of entry:** Pathogens can be more or less effective at causing disease depending on where they enter the body. The entry point is known as the "portal of entry".
